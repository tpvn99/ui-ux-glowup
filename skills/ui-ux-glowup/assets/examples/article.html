Why we stopped using story points

After 14 months of tracking cycle time instead, our median lead time fell from 9 days to 4. Here is what we measured and what we would do again.

Ines Marchetti, engineering lead · September 12, 2026 · 7 min read

On this page

  • What we measure now
  • Fourteen months of results
  • What we would change

In March 2025 our team of eleven spent two hours estimating a sprint. Three weeks later, the biggest ticket, a 13-pointer, shipped in two days, and a 2-pointer took nine. Nobody was surprised, and that was the problem.

The estimate nobody trusted

Points were meant to describe relative effort. In practice they became a negotiation: engineers padded, product managers pushed back, and the number that survived said more about the meeting than about the work.

We pulled the data from the previous 40 sprints. The correlation between estimated points and actual days in progress was 0.31. A coin flip would have been about as useful for planning, and it would have saved us the two hours.

What we measure now

We kept three numbers and dropped everything else: cycle time (first commit to production), work in progress (open pull requests per person) and age of the oldest open item. All three come from the repository, so nobody types anything in.

Planning became a five-minute conversation: "Is this about the size of the last three things we shipped? If it is bigger, can we split it?"

Fourteen months of results

Median lead time dropped from 9 days to 4. The 90th percentile fell from 31 days to 12. Planning time per sprint went from 2 hours to 25 minutes. Customer-reported regressions stayed flat, which was the number we feared most.

What we would change

We removed estimates too abruptly. Two newer engineers missed the shared vocabulary points had given them, and we spent a month rebuilding it with written examples of small, medium and large work. The template we use is here.
